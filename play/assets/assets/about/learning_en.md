# How this app helps you learn

Make Me Smarter is practice: short sets of questions you answer from memory. This page explains what it is built on, and what it can and can't do.

## Answering beats re-reading

Trying to recall an answer strengthens memory more than reading it again: the "testing effect" (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). A large review of study techniques rated practice testing and spaced practice as the two most useful (Dunlosky et al., 2013). So every level is made of questions, and every miss shows the right answer and how to get there.

## Spaced review

A missed question comes back the next day, then after longer and longer gaps: 2, 4, 8 and 16 days for skills, and up to 64 days for exact facts. Spreading practice out works better than cramming it together (Cepeda et al., 2006).

## Mixed practice

"Mix it up" shuffles different kinds of questions together. Mixing makes practice feel harder, but it helps you choose the right method later (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007).

## Trying first

Lessons ask you to try an example before they show the trick. An attempt, even a wrong one, helps the explanation stick (Kornell, Hays & Bjork, 2009).

## How sure are you?

After you pick an answer, you can say how sure you are. Stats then shows whether "Sure" really means right. Confident mistakes come back first in Review, because a mistake that surprises you is easier to correct for good (Butterfield & Metcalfe, 2001).

## Where the techniques come from

- Mental math tricks from "Secrets of Mental Math" (Arthur Benjamin and Michael Shermer) and "Vedic Mathematics" (Bharati Krishna Tirthaji, 1965). The methods are ordinary algebra; historians dispute that they come from the ancient Vedas.
- The memory palace and the major system, as described in "Moonwalking with Einstein" (Joshua Foer).
- Reasoning questions in the spirit of "Thinking, Fast and Slow" (Daniel Kahneman), "How to Solve It" (George Pólya) and Raymond Smullyan's knights and knaves puzzles.
- Habit tips from "Atomic Habits" (James Clear) and "Tiny Habits" (BJ Fogg).

## What it can't do

**Practice makes you faster and more accurate at the kinds of questions you practise**, and the memory techniques help you remember what you use them on. Research has not shown that brain-training games raise IQ, or improve memory, attention or school results in everyday life (Simons et al., 2016). We don't claim they do.
