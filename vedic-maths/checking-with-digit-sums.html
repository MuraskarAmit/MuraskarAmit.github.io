Checking answers with digit sums

गुणितसमुच्चयः Guṇitasamuccayaḥ “the product of the sums”, a sutra of Vedic maths. The book uses this sutra to check algebra by adding up coefficients. Later Vedic maths courses check arithmetic the same way, with digit sums.

A digit sum check tells you in a few seconds whether a multiplication is wrong. It is much older than Vedic maths: Indian mathematicians checked their arithmetic this way by the 10th century, Ibn Sīnā called it the Hindu method, and it reached Europe through Fibonacci’s Liber Abaci in 1202. Tirthaji’s book doesn’t teach it, but Vedic maths courses do, as the way to check every answer.

The method

  1. Find each number’s digit sum: add its digits, and keep adding until one digit is left. 437: 4 + 3 + 7 = 14, then 1 + 4 = 5.
  2. Multiply the two digit sums, and take the digit sum of that: 5 × 8 = 40, which gives 4.
  3. Find the digit sum of the answer: 11362 gives 13, then 4.
  4. If they differ, the answer is wrong. If they match, it is very likely right.
  5. A 9 counts the same as 0, so you can skip 9s, and digits that add up to 9, as you go: that is casting out nines.

Worked examples

Is 437 × 26 = 11362?

  1. Digit sum of 437.4 + 3 + 7 = 14 → 1 + 4 = 5
  2. Digit sum of 26.2 + 6 = 8
  3. Multiply the two, and take the digit sum.5 × 8 = 40 → 4 + 0 = 4
  4. Digit sum of the answer.1 + 1 + 3 + 6 + 2 = 13 → 1 + 3 = 4
  5. Both come to 4, so the answer passes.

437 × 26 = 11362 passes the check, and it is right.

Is 437 × 26 = 11352?

  1. Digit sum of 437.4 + 3 + 7 = 14 → 1 + 4 = 5
  2. Digit sum of 26.2 + 6 = 8
  3. Multiply the two, and take the digit sum.5 × 8 = 40 → 4 + 0 = 4
  4. Digit sum of the answer.1 + 1 + 3 + 5 + 2 = 12 → 1 + 2 = 3
  5. One comes to 4, the other to 3, so the answer is wrong.

437 × 26 = 11352 is wrong. (It is 11362.)

Is 437 × 26 = 11632?

  1. Digit sum of 437.4 + 3 + 7 = 14 → 1 + 4 = 5
  2. Digit sum of 26.2 + 6 = 8
  3. Multiply the two, and take the digit sum.5 × 8 = 40 → 4 + 0 = 4
  4. Digit sum of the answer.1 + 1 + 6 + 3 + 2 = 13 → 1 + 3 = 4
  5. Both come to 4, so the answer passes.

437 × 26 = 11632 passes the check, but it is wrong: the right answer is 11362.

Why it works

10 is one more than 9, so 10, 100, 1000 and every other power of 10 leaves 1 over when divided by 9. So a number leaves the same amount over as its digit sum: 437 = 4 × 100 + 3 × 10 + 7 leaves what 4 + 3 + 7 leaves.

What is left over multiplies along with the numbers: if a leaves 5 and b leaves 8, then a × b leaves what 5 × 8 leaves. A right answer must agree.

The sutra’s own use is the same idea in algebra: (x + 2)(x + 5) = x² + 7x + 10, and the sums of the coefficients agree, 3 × 6 = 18 = 1 + 7 + 10. Adding up coefficients is putting 1 in place of x; a digit sum puts 1 in place of 10, which is what 10 leaves over after taking out 9.

Watch out

  • It misses mistakes that change the answer by a multiple of 9: two swapped digits (11362 and 11632) or a lost 0 give the same digit sum.
  • It works for adding and subtracting too: the digit sums add or subtract the same way.
