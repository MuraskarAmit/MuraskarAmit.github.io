Subtracting from 100, 1000 or 10,000: all from 9 and the last from 10

निखिलं नवतश्चरमं दशतः Nikhilaṃ Navataścaramaṃ Daśataḥ “all from 9 and the last from 10”, a sutra of Vedic maths.

Taking a number away from 1000 usually means borrowing across a row of zeros. The sutra Nikhilaṃ Navataścaramaṃ Daśataḥ, “all from 9 and the last from 10”, skips the borrowing: take every digit from 9, and the last one from 10.

The answer is the number’s complement: how far it is from the round number. You need it for change from a ₹1000 note, and it is the first step of the Vedic ways to multiply near a base.

The method

  1. Give the number as many digits as the round number has zeros: to take 57 from 1000, think of it as 057.
  2. Take each digit from 9, except the last.
  3. Take the last digit from 10.
  4. If the number ends in zeros, they stay zeros, and the last digit that isn’t 0 is the one you take from 10.

Worked examples

1000 − 357

  1. Take each digit from 9, except the last.9 − 3 = 6, 9 − 5 = 4
  2. Take the last digit from 10.10 − 7 = 3

1000 − 357 = 643

10,000 − 4086

  1. Take each digit from 9, except the last.9 − 4 = 5, 9 − 0 = 9, 9 − 8 = 1
  2. Take the last digit from 10.10 − 6 = 4

10,000 − 4086 = 5914

1000 − 270

  1. Take each digit from 9, except the last.9 − 2 = 7
  2. Take the last digit that isn't 0 from 10.10 − 7 = 3
  3. The zeros at the end stay zeros.0

1000 − 270 = 730

Why it works

Taking a number from 1000 is the same as taking it from 999 and adding 1. Taking digits from 9 never needs borrowing, because no digit is bigger than 9: 999 − 357 = 642. Adding the 1 back lands on the last digit, which is the same as taking that digit from 10: 643.

Watch out

  • Short numbers need their zeros in front: 10,000 − 38 is 10,000 − 0038, so 9, 9, 6 and 2: 9962.
  • The round number must be a 1 and zeros. For 5000 − 357, take 357 from 1000 (643) and add the other 4000: 4643.
